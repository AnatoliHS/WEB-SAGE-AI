Evidence-Based Dental Advice.

Discover the science behind common dental hygiene practices. Empowering you to make informed decisions for your daily routine.

Electric vs. Manual Toothbrushes: The Clinical Evidence +

Many patients ask if upgrading to an electric toothbrush is truly worth it. From a clinical perspective, the short answer is yes. Extensive clinical trials show that electric toothbrushes are significantly more effective at removing plaque biofilm and reducing gum disease.

Key Advantages Include:

  • Superior Plaque Removal: High-end electric toothbrushes (specifically sonic or rotating-oscillating types) remove up to 21% more plaque than manual brushes.
  • Pressure Controls: A common mistake with manual brushing is scrubing too hard, which leads to permanent gum recession and enamel wear. Electric toothbrushes have built-in pressure sensors that alert you when brushing too aggressively.
  • Built-in Timers: The average person brushes with a manual toothbrush for only 45 seconds. Electric brushes force a full 2-minute cycle, partitioned into 30-second quad indicators.
Why Flossing is Non-Negotiable (Brushing Only Cleans 60%) +

Brushing only cleans the front, back, and biting surfaces of your teeth. The sides of your teeth—the interdental spaces—make up approximately 40% of your total tooth surface area. If you do not floss, you are leaving almost half of your mouth uncleaned.

Plaque bacteria colony buildup in these tight spaces is the primary cause of:

  • Interproximal Cavities: Cavities that start between the teeth, which are invisible to the eye and require diagnostic X-rays to detect.
  • Interdental Gingivitis: Inflammation of the pointed gum tissue between teeth, leading to bad breath (halitosis) and bleeding.
  • Water Flosser vs String Floss: String floss physically scrapes plaque biofilm off the tooth wall, making it the gold standard. However, if you find string flossing too difficult or tedious, a water flosser is a fantastic alternative that flushes out food debris and bacteria from deep within the gum pockets.
Understanding Gum Disease: Gingivitis vs. Periodontitis +

Gum disease is a progressive, bacterial infection that exists in two primary stages: Gingivitis and Periodontitis. Understanding the difference is crucial for protecting your smile.

Gingivitis (Reversible): This is the initial response to plaque buildup. Gums become red, puffy, and bleed during cleaning. At this stage, the bacteria have not yet affected the bone supporting your teeth. Gingivitis can be fully cured with professional cleanings and improved home care.

Periodontitis (Irreversible): If gingivitis is left untreated, the chronic inflammation causes the gums to pull away from the teeth, creating deep "pockets" where bacteria multiply. The body's immune response to these bacteria begins to break down the surrounding bone that holds your teeth in place. Once bone is lost, it cannot grow back. Treatment shifts from curing the disease to stabilizing it (Periodontal Maintenance) to prevent tooth loss.

What Actually Happens During a Scaling (Deep Cleaning)? +

When plaque (soft bacterial film) remains on your teeth for more than 24-48 hours, it absorbs minerals from your saliva and calcifies into a rock-hard substance called Calculus (Tartar). Once calculus forms, it cannot be brushed or flossed away—it can only be removed by a dental professional.

During your appointment, Sheena performs Scaling and Root Planing:

  • Ultrasonic Scaling: An acoustic handpiece vibrates at high frequency, using water micro-cavitation to break up hard tartar deposits and flush away bacterial toxins.
  • Hand Instruments (Curettes): Finely tuned hand instruments are used to precisely scrape remaining deposits from the root surfaces, leaving them smooth so gum tissue can reattach.
  • Why smooth roots matter: Bacteria easily adhere to rough tartar. By scraping the tooth clean and smoothing the root, we remove the "microbial anchors" and give your gums a clean surface to heal against.

Frequently Asked Questions.

Is charcoal toothpaste safe for daily use? +

Clinical dental professionals do not recommend charcoal toothpaste for daily use. While it may temporarily scrub away surface stains, activated charcoal is highly abrasive. Over time, it acts like sandpaper, wearing down your protective outer enamel. This exposes the yellow dentin underneath, making your teeth look darker and significantly more sensitive.

Why do my teeth feel sensitive after a professional cleaning? +

Calculus (tartar) acts like a thick, insulating blanket over exposed tooth roots. When Sheena scales off this buildup, the underlying root surface is exposed to temperature changes in the mouth for the first time in months. The minor irritation is normal and usually resolves within a week. Using a sensitivity toothpaste helps speed up the recovery process.

How is a dental hygienist (RDH) different from a dentist? +

Dental Hygienists (RDH) and Dentists work as a team, but have different scopes of practice. An RDH is a licensed specialist focused entirely on preventive oral health, gum therapy (scaling), and home-care education. Dentists focus on restorative care, such as diagnostics, fillings, root canals, crowns, and oral surgery. Regular cleanings with your RDH reduce the need for restorative work with your dentist.
